# Build coverage tags on tracking, then video

Your three-stage order (tracking → tags first, video → tracking second, reports third) is correct. What needs fixing is what Stage 1 can learn from the data you have. BDB 2026 frame 1 *is* the snap (confirmed on 9,510 of 9,519 matched tracks), so it supports a strong **shell-at-snap, man/zone and coverage-type model** on 14,108 plays of the 2023 season. It has **no frames before the snap and no pass rushers**, so blitz and pre-snap disguise cannot be learned from it. Those two tags have to come from the 5,215 quality-filtered StatsBomb dropbacks (all 22 players, a median 3.5 s before the snap) and the sparse BDB 2027 labels. The hardest limit is label quality. The NGS-family labels agree with each other on **93%** of plays but with FTN on only **57% exact, 84% on shell and 82% on man/zone**. NGS's own best production model reaches **88.9% on 8 classes and 95.4% on man/zone** with roughly three times your data. So the plan should use rules for geometric tags (shell, box, rushers, rotation), ML for coverage family, and one label source per target, with the FTN-agreeing plays as a clean test set. Before any computer-vision work starts, run a noise-injection experiment on the clean tracking. It shows how much positional error the coverage model tolerates, and that number becomes the accuracy spec for the video pipeline. On the business side, the NFL is already served by NGS, Teamworks/Telemetry (25 teams) and SumerSports. The open gap is defensive coverage and pressure tagging from plain film at the high-school and college level. Only Stage 2 reaches that market, so the tracking model is the engine and the evaluation harness for the product, not the product itself.

## The baseline holds, but blitz and pre-snap disguise need different data

The audit settles the facts that the plan depends on. BDB 2026 covers **all of 2023 REG weeks 1–18: 14,108 pass plays, 272 games**. Frame 1 matches BDB 2027's `ball_snap` event exactly, and the last input frame is always one frame before `pass_forward`. Each play is therefore a snap-to-throw clip of median **26 frames (2.6 s)** at 10 Hz, ranging from 0.8 s to 12.3 s ([a02_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a02_out.txt), [a01_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a01_out.txt)). Every tracked defender has the role "Defensive Coverage", usually 7 per play, and OL and rushers are absent. The roster itself leaks the answer: **11 minus the number of tracked coverage defenders equals the charted rusher count on 88% of plays** (98% within ±1) ([a01_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a01_out.txt)). A blitz model trained on BDB 2026 would learn to count rows, not to read alignment. The sample is also biased toward targeted throws: there are no sacks, scrambles or throwaways without a target.

Here is how each of your five tags maps to the data. **Shell at the snap** works on BDB 2026 frame 1, computed by rule from safety depth and width. **Man/zone and coverage type** are learnable on BDB 2026 with NGS-taxonomy labels: C3 4,482, C1 3,175, C4 2,346, C2Z 1,837, C6 1,388, C0 601, C2M 237, PREVENT 39 ([a08_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a08_out.txt)). **Post-snap rotation** is measurable on BDB 2026 frames 1–N, and BDB 2027's `coverage_assignment_at_snap` versus final `coverage_assignment` gives a direct check: 16% of labeled defenders change, mostly deep-third↔man and quarters→thirds or flats ([a03_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a03_out.txt)). **Pre-snap disguise** (movement before the snap) exists only in StatsBomb. **Blitz** can be learned only from StatsBomb `play_blitz`, which agrees 86% with "five or more rushers" for 2017–2020, or from BDB 2027's sparse 2023 tracking. In BDB 2027, `blitzing` means "rushed the passer" and is True for 92% of EDGE players, so use `blitzing AND lined_up_position NOT IN (EDGE, INTERIOR_LINE)` ([a06_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a06_out.txt), [a03_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a03_out.txt)).

StatsBomb is valuable but narrow. It has 22 tracks on 16,019 of 16,050 plays. Of those, **5,215 labeled dropbacks** have ≥1 s before the snap and all safeties ≥0.9 on camera, and half of them (2,620) have NE/TB on defense. Every one has a Brady offense ([a06_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a06_out.txt)). Off-camera positions are **silently imputed**, not left as gaps, so `on_camera_ratio` must be used as a filter. That is enough data to fine-tune and test a model pretrained on BDB 2026, but too thin to train a 7-class model from scratch. The BDB 2027 per-defender labels are sparser than DATA.md suggests. Only **3,426 of 94,293 BDB 2026 coverage defenders (3.6%)** have an assignment, and no play is fully labeled ([a03_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a03_out.txt)). They work as an evaluation set and a sparse auxiliary target, not as a dense per-defender training target.

## Label noise, not the model, sets the accuracy ceiling

The published bar is high. NGS/AWS trained a CNN with temporal attention on defender × offense pair tensors, using 2018–2020 (about 17k plays per season), and tested on the held-out 2021 season. It scored **88.9% on 8 classes, 97.6% top-2, 95.4% on man/zone** ([AWS ML blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/identifying-defense-coverage-schemes-in-nfls-next-gen-stats/)). The 2026 factorized-attention transformer reaches about **88% on per-defender assignment from pre-snap frames and about 92% with post-snap frames**. Receiver–defender matchups rise from about 75% to 87–89% ([arXiv 2603.25901](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.25901v1)). NGS says label noise "put an artificial ceiling on measured accuracy, which came in around 88%" ([AWS M&E blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/media/reading-the-defense-how-the-nfl-and-aws-quantify-coverage-responsibility)). Results on public data are far weaker. On 3,963 BDB 2025 plays with a 75.2% zone base rate, an HMM plus XGBoost man/zone model reaches about **73% accuracy, AUC 0.80**, which is at or below the majority class ([arXiv 2602.10784](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10784)). This is Ron Yurko's point: report base rates and simple baselines, or the accuracy number means nothing ([Yurko](https://statthinksportsanalytics.substack.com/p/thoughts-on-big-data-bowl-2025)).

The local audit measures how noisy the labels are. BDB 2026 and BDB 2027 `team_coverage_type` agree on **93.1%** of the same plays. NGS-family labels agree with FTN on **56.6–57.3% exact**, **83–84% on shell** and **81–82% on man/zone** ([a03_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a03_out.txt), [a04_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a04_out.txt)). The disagreement is structured. **28.8% of NGS Cover 3 is FTN Cover 1**, which is the pattern-match/MEG overlap AWS saw in its own t-SNE ([AWS ML blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/identifying-defense-coverage-schemes-in-nfls-next-gen-stats/)). Only 21.5% of NGS Cover 6 is FTN Cover 6. NGS-zone/FTN-man disagreements outnumber the reverse 2,915 to 509. FTN's own class mix also drifts: COMBO falls from 1,233 to 77 between 2023 and 2025, and Cover 1 goes 36.5% → 22.5%. FTN seasons are therefore not stationary ([a04_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a04_out.txt)). There is one more uncertainty. NGS's model launched in 2022, so BDB's 2023 labels may be partly NGS model output. A model trained on them may partly learn to imitate NGS, which makes same-source accuracy look better than true accuracy. No source confirms this either way.

That leads to four rules. **Train on one source per target**: NGS-family BDB labels for 2023 tracking, and nflverse 2018–22 NGS labels for StatsBomb. **Never pool** NGS ≤2022 with FTN 2023+. **Carry a `label_source` column** and report metrics per source. **Use the roughly 57% of plays where NGS and FTN agree as a high-confidence test subset**. Treat cross-provider accuracy as a human-human benchmark, not a failure: a model scoring 57% against FTN is matching two professional charting shops. SumerSports uses the same idea as its release gate. It ships a tag only when blind experts "agree with our model at least as often as they agree with each other" ([SumerSports](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/how-sumerlive-tracks-the-game/)).

## Track geometry by rule and coverage by model

The industry split is consistent. Hudl IQ derives formations, fronts, stunts and blitzes from "logic-based rules derived from event frame locations and player tracking", and generates coverage with models ([Hudl](https://www.hudl.com/blog/how-hudl-iq-creates-football-data)). QB coaches teach the same order: safeties first (MOFO/MOFC), then corner leverage and cushion, then box and pressure looks, then motion to confirm man or zone ([Smart Football](https://smartfootball.blogspot.com/2005/12/reading-square-to-determine-coverage.html); [Kim Football](https://kimfootball.substack.com/p/man-vs-zone-who-is-that-defender)). Output those indicators as measured features, not just a final label, so a coach can check them against his own vocabulary. The proposed per-play schema:

| Output | Definition / method | Training or validation source | Realistic ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shell at snap (0/1/2-high) | Rule: DBs deeper than ~9–10 yd and inside the numbers at frame 1 | Hand audit plus NGS-derived shell (single-high 7,657 / two-high 5,808 / zero 601) | Definitional; NGS-derived shell disagrees by the rotation rate |
| Alignment features | Rule: each safety's depth and width, CB cushion and inside/outside shade, press/off, nickel apex, LB depth, box count | BDB 2026 frame 1 | Exact on tracking |
| Man/zone | Model | BDB 2026 `team_coverage_man_zone` | ~95% same-source (NGS); ~82% vs FTN |
| Coverage family (C0, C1, 2-Man, C2, C3, Quarters 4/6, Prevent excluded) | Model; also the 7-class fine label | BDB 2026 `team_coverage_type` | 88.9% 8-class (NGS, 3 seasons); ~57% vs FTN |
| Post-snap rotation | Rule: high-safety count at snap vs snap + ~1 s / throw; model: P(cov \| snap) vs P(cov \| throw) | BDB 2027 at-snap vs final (16% change) | Unpublished; validate against assignment changes |
| Disguise score | 1 − P(true coverage \| snap frame), or KL(pre ‖ post), following NGS's "predict before snap, compare after" ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/de/blogs/media/nfl-next-gen-stats-and-aws-decode-defense-coverage-patterns/)) | Derived | Not a published named metric |
| Rushers, blitz, simulated pressure | Rule on all-22 tracks: count rushers and second-level rushers, derive booleans | StatsBomb `play_blitz` 2017–20; BDB 2027 sparse; FTN `n_blitzers` | ~86% label agreement; context-only floor AUC 0.71 |
| Per-defender assignment | Sparse auxiliary head | 3,426 BDB 2026 defenders; 9,471 in BDB 2027 2023 tracking | ~88% (NGS, dense labels) |

Store counts (`n_rushers`, `n_second_level_rushers`) and derive booleans from them. Define every rate over dropbacks and say so. An independent critique of SumerSports' SūmerBrain found blitz rates computed on different denominators and swapped MOFC/MOFO labels ([Wide Left](https://wideleft.football/p/sumerbrain-claims-to-revolutionize)). One consequence matters for validation. A geometric shell at the snap is *supposed* to disagree with the NGS-derived shell on rotated plays, and PFF measured 30.2% of 2025 snaps with a safety rotation ([PFF](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-preseason-2026-do-nfl-defenses-really-show-their-hand)). Validate the shell rule with a hand audit of film. Then read its disagreement with the label-derived shell as an implied rotation rate, which should fall in a plausible band, rather than as an error.

## A GBM baseline, then a small set transformer, fits a 6 GB GPU

The best-evidenced architecture is a permutation-equivariant transformer over players. SumerSports' open `SportsTrackingTransformer` (PyTorch Lightning, about 100 lines, Zoo baseline included) beat the Zoo pair-CNN on BDB 2024 at **4.95 yd with only 52K parameters versus 5.78 yd**, and it powered the 2025 BDB winner, "Exposing Coverage Tells in the Pre-Snap" ([SumerSports repo](https://github.com/SumerSports/SportsTrackingTransformer); [SumerSports](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/sumersports-big-data-bowl-2026/)). Its license field was empty, so check before reusing the code. Fork it, apply it per frame, add temporal attention over frames, and use NGS's **random truncation** (cut plays at −30/−20/−10/0 frames or at random points, 60/40) so one model can score coverage at any moment ([arXiv 2603.25901](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.25901v1)). Add y-mirroring to double the data. When mirroring, swap the side-specific per-defender labels (DEEP_THIRD_LEFT↔RIGHT). Then add label smoothing and a 5-seed ensemble, as NGS did. Tensors are B × T × ~13 × ~8 features, small enough for the GTX 1660 Super. A full 24-config sweep of the Sumer model took about 8–12 GPU-hours.

Run three baselines before the transformer: majority class (C3 31.8%, zone 71.5%), a **situation-only** model (on 3rd & 1–3, Cover 1 is 41.1%, and on goal-to-go 1st & short, Cover 0 is 77.2%), and **XGBoost on snap-frame alignment features**. These keep the transformer honest. The 2025 BDB honorable mention "Disguised Intentions" used XGBoost with a week 1–5 / 6–9 temporal split ([GitHub](https://github.com/miguelmendesduarte/big-data-bowl-2025)). Split by time: **train weeks 1–12 (9,400 plays), validate 13–15 (2,253), test 16–18 (2,455)**. Add a leave-teams-out fold, and hold out the same games in BDB 2027, since 7,314 plays overlap ([a07_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a07_out.txt)). Several features must never be inputs: the number or identity of tracked defenders, `player_role`, `player_to_predict`, targeted-receiver flags, `dropback_type`, `pass_location_type`, `route_of_targeted_receiver`, and any post-snap frame in a "snap" model. Train with and without n_coverage to confirm the model is not quietly counting rushers.

The headline deliverable should be one no paper has published: **team-coverage accuracy as a function of time after the snap**, from frame 1 to the throw. The pre-snap → post-snap gap NGS reports for per-defender tasks (88 → 92%) suggests most of the man/zone signal arrives in the first second after the snap. The size of that gap *is* your disguise measurement. For blitz on StatsBomb, the floor to beat is a context-only logistic model at **AUC 0.708** (blitz base rate about 25%) ([nfl-blitz-predictor](https://github.com/Kenjaro70/nfl-blitz-predictor)). Before trusting StatsBomb as a deployment proxy, drop its rushers to mimic BDB 2026 and measure the transfer gap between the two input regimes. Video will deliver all 22 players, and BDB 2026 trains on about 13.

## Video is a registration problem before it is a detection problem

The Kaggle numbers do not measure what you need. In the NFL Helmet Assignment competition, the top teams labeled detected helmets *given* ground-truth NGS positions: 3rd place scored **0.904 private LB**, and 1st place's image→bird's-eye mapping plus ICP alone scored **≈0.8**. Registration was solved by fitting detections to known tracking, which a video-only pipeline cannot do ([Kaggle writeups](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Abirate/all_write_ups)). The value of those datasets is the pairing itself: **synchronized sideline and end-zone video at 60 fps with 10 Hz NGS** (60 training plays in Helmet Assignment; Contact Detection adds more, count unconfirmed). That pairing lets you train an image→field model and measure true yard error. Two things are unverified: whether the closed competitions still let you accept the rules and download (DATA.md records that BDB 2021's join button is greyed out), and whether their terms allow commercial reuse. Settle both before committing to Phase 2. Whether those Kaggle camera views match coaching all-22 framing is also unverified.

AMF tracking is materially harder than soccer. The only public AMF MOT benchmark (Keio, college, hand-held) reports **HOTA 45.7 / IDF1 56.1** with fine-tuned BoT-SORT, against 75–85 HOTA for Deep-EIoU-class trackers on soccer and SportsMOT ([arXiv 2511.09455](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09455v1); [Deep-EIoU](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13074)). Football also has structural advantages that soccer pipelines do not use: plays are short, so identity can be solved offline per play with **known 11-vs-11 cardinality**; offense and defense sit on opposite sides of the ball at the snap, which gives team assignment almost free (Kaggle color clustering already hit **97–98%**); and roles can come from alignment, so jersey OCR is unnecessary on wide film. Field registration is the novel engineering. Soccer's HRNet-keypoint + PnL methods reach **0.23–0.60 m** mean projection error ([PnLCalib](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08401v5)), but no AMF model or benchmark exists. Yard lines repeat every 5 yd, so a single frame can be off by exactly ±5 yd multiples. Anchor with yard-number detection, the end zone, or the known line of scrimmage from play-by-play, then propagate the homography across frames. Geometry should drive the camera choice. Sideline all-22 resolves downfield depth (safety depth) well and compresses width. End-zone film resolves width and leverage and compresses depth. So start with sideline all-22 as the primary view.

The 1660 Super is adequate for inference. The estimate is **YOLO11s at 1280 px running ~10–25 FPS** with TensorRT FP16, unmeasured, so benchmark it. A 60-play game at 10 Hz becomes a 10–40 minute batch job ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo11/)). Rent an RTX 4090 at **$0.14–0.74/hr** for detector and keypoint training, and for any SAM2 or VLM pass, which will not fit 6 GB at video scale ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-cloud-pricing-comparison-runpod-vs-vastai-2026/)). Use the Mac for annotation (CVAT) and post-processing. No study has measured how video-derived tracking error degrades AMF coverage models, and a soccer GSR team found a role classifier trained on clean positions underperformed on detected ones ([SoccerNet 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10587)). So train the Phase 1 model with injected noise: σ 0.5–1.5 yd jitter (heavier along the camera's depth axis), dropouts, and same-team ID swaps. The working spec, to be confirmed by your own noise curve, is **≤1 yd mean field error (≤0.5 yd lateral), ≥95% of DB/WR frames tracked, and under one DB/WR identity switch per play**. Man/zone will tolerate more error than Cover 2 vs Cover 4, which turns on safety depth.

There is also an IP and market check to do. US patents already cover snap detection, field-line reconstruction and player geolocation "within video of American football" for levels "from high school through professional" ([USPTO 12315258](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12315258); [USPTO 12367676](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12367676)). The assignee could not be extracted, so get a freedom-to-operate review before selling. Being based in British Columbia also raises a market question the notes did not research. Canadian football uses a larger field and 12 players a side, so a CFL or U Sports product would need its own field template and a 12-man model.

## Reports win on conditional tendencies with n, reliability and clips

Coaches act on short prioritized lists ("top five" pressures), conditional tendencies in defined down-and-distance buckets, and one-click video for every claim ([Hudl](https://www.hudl.com/blog/scouting-and-gameplanning-for-defensive-pressures); [Teamworks](https://teamworks.com/coaching-nfl)). Each report line should therefore read: condition → outcome distribution → n → reliability → clips. An example: "2-high, 3rd & 6–10, 11 personnel: rotates to Cover 3 58%, Quarters 24% (n = 33, ±17 pp) [8 clips]." Shrink small cells toward league or team base rates and grey out cells with n < 10. Show a **disguise-risk column** so the QB knows when to wait for the post-snap read, and a **tell-reliability** line (precision, firing rate, lift over base rate). Disguise is the dominant modern problem. Two-high looks on passes rose from 44% (2019) to 63% (2024) ([NFL.com](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2024-nfl-season)). The 2025 Eagles showed two-high on about 87% of plays and disguised 41% of them, 98% open-to-closed ([MatchQuarters](https://www.matchquarters.com/p/fangio-cover-9-eagles-two-high-disguise)). Exclude preseason by default: league Cover 1 was 25.6% in preseason versus 17.6% in the regular season ([PFF](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-preseason-2026-do-nfl-defenses-really-show-their-hand)). Sample sizes constrain everything. A team-season has 478–734 labeled dropbacks, and only 119–238 on 3rd and 4th down, so Cover 0, Cover 6 and 2-Man tendencies need pooling or shrinkage ([a04_out.txt](../scratch/audit/a04_out.txt)).

A report v0 can be built in week one from nflverse charting alone (coverage, man/zone, rushers and blitzers by situation per opponent). That fixes the product's shape early and shows exactly which columns the models add: shell at the snap, disguise, alignment tells. The positioning follows from the market. In the NFL, Telemetry/Teamworks is used by 25 teams and already analyzes "defensive shell disguises and rotation patterns" ([Teamworks](https://teamworks.com/coaching-nfl)). Hudl IQ claims automatic coverage and blitz tagging for college ([Hudl IQ](https://www.hudl.com/products/football-iq)). Hudl Assist's documented auto-tags for high schools are offense-centric ([Hudl Assist](https://www.hudl.com/products/assist/football)). The open wedge is defensive coverage and pressure tagging from ordinary sideline or end-zone film for high-school and lower-college staffs, who work from 3–4 games of film (about 150–250 snaps). That is exactly where shrinkage and explicit n matter most.

## Conclusion

The project depends less on modeling skill than on measurement discipline. Once the label ceilings are known, a 52K-parameter transformer on a 6 GB card can approach what matters. The contributions that would be new and defensible are the accuracy-versus-time-after-snap curve, a disguise score grounded in that curve, and the noise-tolerance curve that turns "good enough video tracking" from a guess into a spec. That last one is the bridge between your stages. It should exist before a single frame of film is annotated, because it decides whether the video pipeline needs half-yard precision or can live with a yard and a half.

The commercial logic runs in the opposite direction from the build order. The tracking model is easy to build and hard to sell, because NGS, Telemetry and SumerSports already own the NFL tracking-to-tags market. The video pipeline is hard to build, but it is the only route into the high-school and college defensive-tagging gap. Two unresolved questions decide whether this can become a company: whether Kaggle's paired data and the BDB training data can legally underpin a commercial model, and whether the existing AMF video patents leave room to operate. Answer both before Phase 3 rather than after.
